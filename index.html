Slide 05 — Customer Discovery (Channels — Key Pivot)
CHANNELS: OUR BIGGEST PIVOT
Biggest channel assumption directly contradicted by interviews → Pivot: Awareness
Day 1 — Hypothesis (Not Validated)
  • Instagram Reels + TikTok short-form content
  • Campus ambassador social posts
  • Assumed: students discover platforms via algorithm-driven feeds
  • Scalable & low-cost to produce
  • → Not validated by interviews
→
Day 10 — Evidence (Adopted)
  • WhatsApp groups + peer referrals
  • Students rely on existing trusted communities
  • Direct recommendations > algorithm discovery
  • Discovery is social & trust-dependent, not ad-driven
  • Network effects = built-in, not paid for ✓

Why It Changed

Interviews revealed students don't trust platforms discovered through ads. They adopt tools recommended by someone they know — a classmate, a group chat, a friend selling furniture.

Decision: PIVOT

Drop Instagram/TikTok outreach entirely for launch. Invest in campus ambassador network and WhatsApp seeding. Deprioritise paid/organic social content.

Slide 08 — A/B Test #1
A/B TEST #1 — VALUE PROPOSITION FRAMING
DESIGN Written & dated BEFORE execution
📅 April 17, 2026 — 9:00am
Element tested

Message framing of the Value Proposition headline shown to potential users

Version A — Control

"Buy & sell second-hand goods on campus" — generic marketplace headline, no trust language

Version B — Variant (one change only)

"Trade with verified students only — no scams, no ghosting" — trust-first, explicit safety promise

Hypothesis

"We believe Version B will outperform Version A on sign-up intent rate because students' primary pain point is trust & accountability, not access to a marketplace."

Success metric
Sign-up intent %
Sample plan
10 students/version
Split method
Random, in-person
Timeline
Apr 18–19, 2 days
EXECUTION
▶ Run: April 18–19, 2026

Approached 20 Bocconi students individually on campus (library, cafeteria). Each was shown a single version of the headline on a phone screen and asked: "Would you sign up for this platform?"

Students were split randomly (first 10 → version A, next 10 → version B). No follow-up questions during the test to avoid priming.

Deviations from plan

None. Test ran exactly as planned. All 20 students responded. No interpretation bias — responses recorded immediately as Yes/No.

Slide 09 — A/B Test #2
A/B TEST #2 — ACQUISITION CHANNEL
DESIGN Written & dated BEFORE execution
📅 April 18, 2026 — 10:30am
Element tested

Acquisition channel used to reach new users — the exact channel, format, and messenger

Version A — Control

Cold Instagram DM from the UniSwap account to Bocconi students found via public profiles

Version B — Variant (one change only: channel)

Message seeded by a trusted campus ambassador inside an existing Bocconi WhatsApp student group

Hypothesis

"We believe Version B will generate a higher 24h response rate because it leverages existing trust networks — a peer recommending it — rather than cold outreach from an unknown account."

Success metric
24h response rate
Sample plan
15 students/version
Split method
Sequential, same message
Timeline
Apr 19–21, 3 days
EXECUTION
▶ Run: April 19–21, 2026

Version A: Sent 15 cold Instagram DMs from the UniSwap student account to Bocconi students found via hashtags and public profiles. Same introductory message for all.

Version B: A campus ambassador dropped the same text message into a Bocconi student WhatsApp group (approx. 80 members). We tracked which of the next 15 replies engaged with the concept.

Deviations from plan

WhatsApp group had 80 members but we tracked only first 15 replies to maintain equal sample size. This slightly underrepresents the full reach of Version B — actual reach was broader.

Slide 10 — A/B Test #3
A/B TEST #3 — PRICING COMMUNICATION
DESIGN Written & dated BEFORE execution
📅 April 19, 2026 — 2:00pm
Element tested

How the transaction fee is framed and communicated to potential users at point of sign-up

Version A — Control

"UniSwap charges a 5% transaction fee" — abstract percentage, standard marketplace language, no explanation of value received

Version B — Variant (one change only: framing)

"Pay €1–2 per transaction for a verified, scam-free exchange" — concrete amount, anchored to specific value

Hypothesis

"We believe Version B will increase willingness to pay because anchoring the fee to a concrete benefit (safety, verification) reduces fee aversion vs. an abstract percentage."

Success metric
"I'd pay this" rate
Sample plan
10 students/version
Split method
Random, in-person
Timeline
Apr 20–22, 3 days
EXECUTION
▶ Run: April 20–22, 2026

Approached 20 Bocconi students individually in the campus cafeteria. After describing UniSwap briefly and uniformly, we showed each student a single card with the fee description (either A or B) and asked: "Would you be willing to pay this?"

Students were randomly split. Same product description before showing fee card. Responses recorded immediately — Yes/No only, no follow-up probing during the test.

Deviations from plan

None material. One student in Version A asked for clarification on the fee base — we restated the question without extra info. This response was counted as "No" to be conservative.

Even Version A's 40% shows latent willingness — but Version B's framing turns "fee aversion" into "value exchange." Students already using free platforms need to see what they're getting, not just what they're paying.

This tells us: the price is not the barrier — the framing is. We do not need to lower fees; we need to anchor them to the trust value UniSwap delivers on every transaction.

🟡 ITERATE

Keep value-anchored framing. Re-test in next sprint with different benefit anchors (e.g. "verified student profile" vs. "scam-free guarantee") to identify which element of trust drives payment most.
